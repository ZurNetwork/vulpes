The internet and its systems are some of the most intricate ones ever created. We participate in several million different ways without even noticing. We create, we consume; we admire, we comment. The internet has become a huge collection of spaces that all claim your attention, all the time, without breaks or excuses. Likewise, we've learned to exist as several different identities everywhere—we become a collection of several different ways to behave depending on where and why we are on a platform. That, by itself, is fine. We have always been many selves. What's broken is that not one of those selves belongs to us.

But—what happens when a platform suddenly changes? We have learned to accept that a platform owning our identity is normal and expected. We log in everywhere through Google's OAuth. Twitter (X?) can ban you just because—even shadowban you if they so desire. Your favorite Telegram chat's admin can decide you are no longer part of the community and cut you off from all your friends, unilaterally.

Isn't this annoying? Isn't this infuriating? Doesn't the unfairness and injustice of it wake up a fire inside of you that makes everything else feel minute in comparison? I believe so.

Not only do I believe so, but I am also tired of this. I am tired of power centralizing itself upon individuals. I am tired of some people and systems deciding how I exist without better reason than "Yeah, no". I am not doing anything immoral; I am not doing anything despicable. I am not hurting anybody, nor am I creating trouble in any way. I merely exist to interact with people in different ways.

This line of thought happened after a Channel on Telegram I owned was banned just like that. It ended up being an erroneous ban as well, which made it all the more infuriating—a mistake, and still no appeal, no recourse, no consent. I had been working on that channel for the past 4 or so years, and it was gone in a second. In simple terms, I was frustrated and angry.

And that is how I started working on decentralization as one of my biggest motivators for what I do. I started working on an application called Zurfur, built on top of the AT Protocol, following BlueSky's footsteps—however, not everything was sunshine and rainbows. As I was falling in love with the complete independence from centralized systems, I kept falling into the same problem: How do you know that someone is telling the truth?

In Guatemala—my country of origin—we have a concept called "Conectes". Conectes translates literally to "connections" in English. When you have conectes, you get someone telling someone else how you are. You may get a job because your Conecte told someone else that you were an incredible engineer. You got a gig because a Conecte told a company that you are an incredible contractor. It is always "This person claims this about me"—and whoever wants to believe it, believes it. This handshake, so ingrained into Guatemalan culture, is partially what inspired a protocol I've come to call the ACP—the Attested Claims Protocol.

Conectes have a dark side, too: doors that only open if you happen to know the right people, whispers nobody can inspect. The protocol's job is to keep the handshake and lose the gatekeeping—every vouch out in the open, and anyone free to become a voucher.

The Attested Claims Protocol doesn't claim to be the all-knowing centralized protocol we've come to expect from applications. "I say you are because I say you are and everybody has to listen to me" simply doesn't work here.

The ACP works like this: You state facts about yourself in a space you hold the keys to; anybody who checks a fact can sign a vouch for it, which you keep. Anybody else can verify who vouched, for what, and until when. And whoever is checking the vouch does not have to ask a central service—or even the voucher themselves. The voucher can disappear entirely, and the vouch still stands.

That is the magic of the system. An app wants to verify that you are +18? Someone vouches for you. But what if whoever is vouching for you is just a friend? The app is free to say "we don't accept that voucher—we accept these", and ask for one it trusts. Nobody rules on truth from the center; every party decides for itself. And those vouches live with you. They are consensual, and nobody can vouch for you on something you don't want, either.

This is the discipline we hold ourselves to, and the one test we ask you to hold us to: if we were to disappear—if anybody were to disappear—would that be an inconvenience, or a breaking factor? We choose our disappearance to be an inconvenience, not a death of your identities.
